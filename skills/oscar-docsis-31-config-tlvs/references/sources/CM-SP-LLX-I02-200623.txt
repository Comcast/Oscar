1 SCOPE
1.1        Introduction and Purpose
The ongoing expansion of mobile networks, particularly with LTE and 5G small cells, increases the demand for
transport networks to provide connectivity for the mobile network elements. The architecture and requirements
defined in this specification enable cable operators to take advantage of their rich infrastructure assets to provide
economical and high-performance transport network services in support of either their own mobile traffic or
backhaul or fronthaul services provided to other mobile operators.
This specification describes techniques for supporting quality of service (QoS) requirements and reducing upstream
latency of mobile traffic while traversing the DOCSIS network. The specification includes three key components:
      1.    an innovative approach of coordinating the scheduling functions of mobile and DOCSIS networks by using
            the bandwidth report (BWR) protocol is specified to reduce the end-to-end latency of mobile traffic;
      2.    a common QoS framework is defined for mapping the QoS configuration from the mobile network to the
            DOCSIS network to enable consistent end-to-end QoS treatment of mobile traffic;
      3.    the CM grant sharing feature that enables real-time grant sharing decisions made by a CM is introduced to
            provide the lowest latency to the highest priority traffic and to enable efficient DOCSIS bandwidth
            utilization under certain network conditions.
While a common QoS framework is necessary to provide consistent end-to-end QoS treatment of mobile traffic, it is
not required that the QoS mapping follow the specific recommendations as detailed in Section 5.3. However, it is
recommended that the DOCSIS network be aware of mobile QoS.
The CM grant sharing is an optional DOCSIS 3.1 and DOCSIS 4.0 feature. The requirements are detailed in
[MULPI] as the “enhanced hierarchical QoS,” or EHQoS, as well as the associated “Absolute Queue-Depth Based
Request mechanism,” also detailed in MULPI. There are two strategies in deploying LLX Services, one involves
CM grant sharing, one without CM grant sharing.
Techniques described in this specification apply equally to backhaul, midhaul, and fronthaul (collectively referred to
as "xhaul") mobile traffic over the DOCSIS transport network.
Although the techniques described in this document can be applied to other access network types, such as Ethernet
passive optical network (EPON), the focus of this specification is on defining the requirements on DOCSIS and
mobile network equipment. In a separate but related effort, work is ongoing at the O-RAN Alliance to promote
commonality with coordinated scheduling solutions proposed for DOCSIS networks (as specified in this
specification) and PON networks.
Other aspects of mobile xhaul such as carrying precision frequency and phase synchronization signals using
DOCSIS technology are addressed separately in the [SYNC] specification.
It is assumed that the DOCSIS equipment is designed to meet the DOCSIS 3.0 or DOCSIS 3.1 suite of specifications.
The techniques described in this specification are independent of the Low Latency DOCSIS (LLD) technique
described in the [MULPI] specification. In the foreseeable future, this Low Latency Mobile Xhaul (LLX)
specification is best suited to support low latency xhaul for today’s mobile networks, including LTE and 5G.
This specification is organized as follows.
      •     Section 5 provides a detailed technical overview of the QoS-matching and latency-reduction technologies,
            i.e., the BWR protocol. It is for informational purposes.
      •     Section 6 details the BWR protocol operations. It is normative.
      •     Section 7 details the message formats of the BWR messages. It is normative.

3 TERMS AND DEFINITIONS
This specification uses the following terms.
  backhaul           In a cellular network, the portion of network that runs between the eNB to the mobile core network

  fronthaul          The network link between the remote radio heads at the cell sites and the centralized baseband controller


  macrocell          A cell in cellular networks that provides radio coverage served by a powered cellular base station

  eNodeB (eNB)       4G LTE base station

  gNodeB (gNB)       5G base station

  small cell         A low-powered cellular radio access node that operates in licensed or unlicensed spectrum that have a range
                     of 10 meters to a few hundred meters
  xhaul              Refers to backhaul, midhaul, and fronthaul

5 OVERVIEW
This section is informative and explains the system architectures, the coordinated scheduling technique for latency
reduction, i.e., the bandwidth report (BWR) technology, and the common QoS framework for mobile and the
DOCSIS xhaul transport.
The latency reduction technique and the common QoS framework specified in this specification are applicable to
fronthaul, midhaul, and backhaul scenarios. For simplicity, the term "xhaul" is used throughout this document to
generically refer to all transport network classes.
To understand how the BWR protocol works and its design considerations, the first use case of backhauling an LTE
eNB (long-term evolution eNodeB) will be examined in detail in the following sections. BWR in the fronthaul
scenario is addressed later in this section after the details of the interface have been explained. The techniques that are
explained in detail for the LTE network examples are generally applicable to other technologies such as 5G and
scheduled Wi-Fi. The BWR protocol specified in Sections 6 and 7 is designed for all xhaul use cases.
The BWR protocol was first documented in [BWR SCTE], and is further documented in [Mobile Xhaul Blueprint].

5.2     The Bandwidth Report (BWR) Concept
The request-grant time is much longer in LTE than in a DOCSIS system, but mobile operators often demand
backhaul delays to be on the order of a few milliseconds. This high latency in LTE conceals a hidden opportunity,
namely, using the long LTE latency to start DOCSIS processes in parallel and reduce the overall LTE-DOCSIS
system latency. This concept, made possible through use of the BWR message, is shown in Figure 6.
Current LTE-DOCSIS systems, which do not have BWR, have a cumulative latency, as shown in the upper diagram
of Figure 6. When the BWR is introduced into the LTE-DOCSIS system, as shown in the lower diagram of Figure 6,
the DOCSIS request-grant loop can be started earlier and in parallel with the LTE request-grant loop, leading to
much lower latency. This is accomplished by treating the LTE and DOCSIS systems as one pipelined system rather
than two independent systems, as they are treated today. For this pipeline mechanism to work, the DOCSIS side
needs information from the LTE side in the form of the BWR message.




                             Figure 6 - Reducing DOCSIS Effective Latency Under LTE



5.2.1    How Does BWR Work?
The BWR allows an external system, such as an LTE eNB, to request bandwidth from a DOCSIS system for some
specific time in the future, before the arrival of the actual traffic. The eNB provides a future traffic profile through
the BWR message, allowing the CMTS to make QoS and granting decisions earlier than it normally would.




06/23/20                                               CableLabs                                                      21
CM-SP-LLX-I02-200623                                           Data Over Cable Service Interface Specification


The BWR replaces the CM’s internal layer 2 request message for LTE uplink data arriving at the CM. The BWR
message itself is an external layer 3 IP-based message that is transmitted from the eNB, transparently through the
CM, to the CMTS. The content of the BWR is populated with information describing data that will arrive at the CM
in the future.
The BWR is created by the BWR client just after the LTE uplink (UL) scheduler finishes granting the UE(s) based
on the UE’s outstanding UL data buffer sizes. The BWR message created after scheduling contains a description of
the amount of UL data that is expected to arrive at the eNB and be forwarded to the CM. The BWR message is then
transmitted from the eNB to the CMTS, through the CM, in a dedicated upstream service flow earlier than the CM
would normally issue its request. As stated in Sections 5.3.1 and 5.3.2, the BWR client can include information from
other LTE status blocks in the BWR message.
The result of using the BWR is that the DOCSIS scheduler is made aware of the LTE scheduler’s scheduling
decisions using the BWR message. Rather than having two separate and sequential latency-additive LTE and
DOCSIS request-grant loops, the BWR starts the DOCSIS request-grant loop early and in parallel to LTE’s uplink
granting. The CMTS scheduler grants the CM just in time for the UE’s upstream data to arrive at the CM, with the
effect of reducing upstream latency. This process is illustrated in Figure 7.




                                    Figure 7 - Pipelining Requests and Grants

striking. In both systems, the UE/CM generates a request when it has data to send, and the scheduler in the
eNB/CMTS responds with a grant.

To adapt the BWR message to the traffic flow, a prediction algorithm is used to monitor the operation of the eNB
scheduler. The BWR client looks at the status of the data path to see if there are any changes in behavior and then
issues the BWR message. The optimal period for the BWR for LTE frequency-division duplex (FDD) is 1 ms
because the eNB scheduler makes scheduling decision once every LTE subframe (every 1 ms). The CMTS uses the
information given in the BWR messages, along with other scheduling constraints, and generates a grant(s) for the CM.
BWR Example: If a 1,000-byte data transfer from the UE will occur in 8 ms and there is an anticipated 1 ms delay
in the eNB software stack for transport block decoding and packet reassembly before transmission to the CM, then a
BWR will be transmitted to the CMTS to request at 1,000 bytes in 9 ms. The CMTS could be configured to add an
additional 2 ms to the grants generated based on the BWR in case there is any delay in traffic leaving the eNB.
Therefore, the CMTS would issue a DOCSIS grant 11 ms after the BWR message is created.

5.2.4.1   Brief Background on the O-RAN Fronthaul Interface
[ORAN CUS] defines the O-RAN distributed unit (O-DU) as a logical node hosting RLC/MAC/high-PHY layers
based on a lower layer functional split; it defines the O-RAN radio unit (O-RU) as a logical node hosting low-PHY
layer and RF processing on a lower layer functional split. An O-DU controls one or more O-RUs. For each LTE or
5G slot, the O-DU sends scheduling and beamforming commands to the O-RU in the form of O-RAN control plane
(c-plane) message(s). The O-RU sends UL IQ data to the O-DU and receives DL IQ data from the O-DU, one
OFDM symbol at a time. See the section on the c-plane protocol in [ORAN CUS] for more details on this process.
The user plane (u-plane) data are encapsulated in eCPRI or RoE headers, which are encapsulated in a "section," as
defined by O-RAN (Figure 12). A section defines the characteristics of the u-plane data to be transferred from a
beam. One or multiple sections can exist for each OFDM symbol.
In addition to the u-plane, the fronthaul is also used to carry control and management plane messages exchanged
between the O-DU and the O-RU.

Figure 13 illustrates how BWR works in the O-RAN fronthaul interface.

The message exchanges are as follows.
    1.   O-DU determines UE grants for slot n.
    2.   O-DU sends the BWR message to the CMTS to request fronthaul bytes for slot n.
    3.   O-DU sends DL u-plane and c-plane data for slot n.
    4.   O-DU sends c-plane message to the O-RU indicating commands for reception of slot n.
    5.   Upon receiving UL UE data in symbols of slot n, O-RU sends u-plane (and possibly c-plane) data for slot
         n. Data is then buffered in the CM until step 6.
    6.   CMTS grants based on the BWR message.
    7.   CM transports u-plane (and possibly c-plane) data to the CMTS.
    8.   CMTS transports u-plane (and possibly c-plane) data to the O-DU.
Step 2 can occur independent of the time of steps 3 and 4. Steps 3 and 4 can occur around the same time in any
order. Step 5 occurs for every OFDM symbol carrying UL data. The frequency of occurrence for steps 6 and 7
depends on CMTS implementation.
Because the O-RU sends the IQ data in the form of u-plane packets for every OFDM symbol rather than queuing the
data and sending them at the end of the slot, the CMTS can schedule DOCSIS grants each time u-plane packets
arrive at the CM. Doing so reduces the latency on the DOCSIS link compared to when grants are scheduled only at
the end of a slot. The frequency of the DOCSIS grant is up to the CMTS implementation and depends in part on the
granularity of the CMTS scheduling interval.
The u-plane packets sent from the O-RU to the CM form a traffic pattern. This pattern can be either explicitly
communicated from the O-DU to the CMTS or derived by the CMTS based on the slot parameters (e.g., slot
duration, number of symbols). The current version of the BWR message is required to include the requested bytes
with a minimum granularity of a slot and allows for an optional pattern descriptor extension to describe the traffic
pattern at a more granular level than a slot. The pattern descriptor allows for varying burst sizes per symbol.
Without the optional pattern descriptor extension, the CMTS can still derive the granular traffic pattern within a slot
because the O-DU schedules the air interface for the entire slot duration, and thus the burst size for each OFDM
symbol period can be assumed to be constant within a slot. Using the bytes requested for a slot in the BWR message



06/23/20                                             CableLabs                                                        27
CM-SP-LLX-I02-200623                                             Data Over Cable Service Interface Specification


and the number of symbols per slot, the CMTS can derive per-symbol burst size at the O-RU. Several factors can
potentially contribute to a traffic pattern that cannot be accurately derived by the CMTS within a slot.
     •   Real-time change of the compression parameter—In theory, the [ORAN CUS] specification allows the
         O-DU to change compression parameter per symbol, which can result in varying burst size sent by the
         O-RU. Although allowed, the flexibility is rarely used by the O-DU.
     •   Time-division duplex (TDD)—Within a slot, downlink and uplink transmissions can switch on a symbol
         boundary, resulting in some symbol periods having zero uplink bursts rather than equal-sized bursts for all
         symbols in the slot. TDD configuration is static; it can be communicated to the CMTS implicitly or
         explicitly via the pattern descriptor.
     •   Losing a c-plane message that describes commands for the slot for the O-RU can lead to part of the UL
         u-plane packets not being transmitted. The loss of a c-plane message can be considered a corner case that
         rarely occurs. Even so, the DOCSIS grant will be unused for the intended traffic, which is acceptable and
         manageable.

As in the backhaul scenario, UE requests bytes in terms of logical channel groups (LCGs). The O-DU schedules a
slot with LCG awareness and can indicate bytes per LCG in the BWR message. [ORAN CUS] uses the concept of a
"section" to encapsulate and transmit a group of IQ samples with the same characteristics. Therefore, the IQ data
from an LCG can be encapsulated into a section, separate from the IQ data from a different LCG, which is placed
into a separate section. Different sections are packetized separately by the O-RU on the fronthaul transport. To
distinguish the sections, the O-DU needs to indicate to the O-RU to encapsulate IQ data belonging to different LCGs
in different sections along with the corresponding DSCP or class of service (CoS) for that section through c-plane
messages. The mapping between the LCG and the DSCP is preconfigured (see Annex C.1). When the O-RU sends
u-plane bursts, the O-RU needs to indicate the DSCP or CoS for that section in either the reserved field or an
extension field in the section header. Upon receiving the O-RAN fronthaul packets, the CM classifies the packets
into DOCSIS SFs based on the DSCP.

predicted by the O-DU scheduler. In addition, [ORAN CUS] requires the c-plane and m-plane messages to be tagged
with DSCP and/or CoS and provides a set of default values. A common set of values needs to be preconfigured on the
CMTS and be communicated to the CM as a set of DOCSIS classifiers, as well as on the O-DU and be communicated
to the O-RU, in order to ensure the fronthaul QoS is matched by the DOCSIS network. See Section 5.3 for details on
the common QoS framework between mobile and DOCSIS networks.

5.2.5    BWR and 5G NR
The LTE internal scheduling time is 8 ms or more. That duration provides enough time for the BWR message to be
generated and delivered to the CMTS before the packets arrive at the CM.
5G NR defines a number of slot duration configurations, including 1 ms, 500 µs, 250 µs, and 125 µs. The smaller
slot durations were defined to accommodate ultra-reliable low latency communication (URLLC).
BWR works in 5G NR similarly to the way it works in LTE for the majority of 5G traffic that employ larger slot
sizes. However, with the small slot duration configurations, the internal scheduling time could be 1–2 ms, so the
BWR message would arrive at the CMTS scheduler after the data arrive at the CM in the backhaul case.
To accommodate this, the CMTS scheduler will have to predict the number of grants required per queue, verify this
a few milliseconds later when the BWR arrives, and adjust as necessary.
More details will be added when 5G NR is available for investigation.

5.3       Common QoS Framework for Mobile and DOCSIS Xhaul Transport
5.3.1      QoS Model of Mobile System with DOCSIS Xhaul
This section discusses the LTE network example and the flow of packets from a source in the user equipment (UE)
to a destination in the evolved packet core (EPC) just past the CMTS. The system diagram in Figure 16 depicts a
simplified LTE-DOCSIS network. The LTE radio access network (RAN) is connected to the EPC using hybrid
fiber-coax (HFC) as transport. The LTE system consists of the UE and the eNB, which are each composed of their
respective subsystems. The DOCSIS system consists of the CM and the CMTS. For simplicity, this discussion will
not go into the detail of the DOCSIS subsystems, which include the CM and the CMTS. The EPC consists of the
SGW (Serving Gateway), PGW (PDN (packet data network) Gateway), and mobility management entity (MME).




                              Figure 16 - QoS Model of LTE System with DOCSIS Xhaul


In this model, three interfaces are involved:
      •    air,
      •    Ethernet (twisted pair or optical), and
      •    HFC.
The air interface is a layer 2 interface internal to the LTE system. The HFC interface is also a layer 2 interface and is
internal to the DOCSIS system. The Ethernet interface, however, is a layer 3 interface when combined with IP. As a
result, QoS needs to be maintained not only between the LTE system and the DOCSIS system but also between
layer 2 and layer 3.
Starting on the left side of Figure 16, there are several applications in the UE that initiate data flows. Any number of
applications can span the classic realms of data, voice, video, and signaling, and each of them typically generate
TCP or UDP flows. The LTE system maps these applications into evolved packet system (EPS) bearers and assigns
each of these flows a QoS class indicator (QCI).
The EPS bearers are mapped into logical channels (LCs). One or more logical channels are collectively mapped into
an LCG to facilitate efficient use of bandwidth for UE bandwidth requests on the radio link. This is important as
there is only a maximum of four LCGs but there can be more than four EPS bearers per UE. UE signaling is
included in one of these LCGs, typically LCG 0. The LTE buffer status report from the UE is based on LCGs.
Note that the grant from the eNB is a single grant for all four LCGs but is subject to a set of rules that will align the
grant with the original request while allowing for newly arrived higher priority packets to be accommodated.
If the grant is not enough to cover the request, then the UE may fragment packets across its air interface. The
segments are received at the eNB and reassembled into packets. The eNB treats each EPS flow differently, which
can result in different delays for different QCI streams. The eNB encapsulates each flow into an S1 GTP (GPRS
Tunneling Protocol) tunnel. Each GTP tunnel is assigned a DiffServ code point (DSCP) for IP quality of service
based on the QCI that was assigned to the EPS bearer. The GTP tunnel is what the CM sees, not the LCG.


30                                                     CableLabs                                               06/23/20
Low Latency Mobile Xhaul over DOCSIS® Technology                                           CM-SP-LLX-I02-200623


When the GTP packets arrive at the DOCSIS CM, they are mapped to a service flow by using a classifier. When a
grant arrives at the CM, the GTP packet is sent to the CMTS. The CMTS forwards the packet across an IP network,
and the GTP packet eventually terminates at the PGW of the EPC. From there, the contents of the GTP packet,
which are the original packets from the application in the UE, are forwarded on to their final destination.
The new additions to this system are on the control plane. The eNB prediction algorithm gathers information from
the LTE scheduler, the HARQ process, the reassembly engine, and other status blocks to generate a BWR message.
The BWR message is a layer 3 request message intended to describe future egress traffic behavior on the Ethernet
port of the eNB. The BWR message is received at the CM and classified to a service flow. When a grant arrives for
that service flow, the BWR message is transported to the CMTS and delivered to a CMTS predictor algorithm that
decodes the BWR information and decides when in the future grants from the DOCSIS CMTS should be sent to the
DOCSIS CM on a particular service flow.

5.3.2    Signaling Traffic
In addition to the UE traffic, there may be S1-AP signaling traffic originating at the eNB and X2 traffic such as
eICIC and CoMP. Signaling traffic that originates at the UE is accounted for in the BWR message since that
signaling will traverse the LCG path and is accounted for in the eNB grants. Signaling traffic that is generated by the
eNB in real-time is not accounted for by the LTE scheduler but can be accounted for by the BWR client. In the
former case, the eNB real-time signaling traffic is not included in the BWR message and requires separate QoS
treatment at the CM. In the latter case, the signaling traffic is included in the BWR message.
Inter-eNB handover is an example of X2-AP signaling, transported using the SCTP protocol. eICIC and CoMP are
other examples of X2-AP signaling traffic. S1-AP and X2-AP latency is unspecified and is generally dictated by the
mobile operator. The general industry understanding is that the desired latency needs to be about 10 ms for inter-eNB
handover and 5 ms or less for CoMP to be able to show any significant performance gain. To achieve this 5 ms
latency budget for CoMP when each eNB is behind a separate CM, the proposed latency distribution would be 2 ms
for the DOCSIS upstream, 1 ms for the DOCSIS downstream, and 2 ms for the rest of the network. If QoS is
observed throughout the entire network path, this latency target should be achievable.

5.3.3    Mobile and DOCSIS Xhaul Interface
This section describes how the traffic flows that the AE receives from the UE are subsequently delivered to the
DOCSIS system while maintaining a common QoS framework between the AE system and the DOCSIS system.
Figure 17 provides an example of the general approach along with several alternative approaches.




                     Figure 17 - Common QoS Framework for Mobile and DOCSIS Networks


Packets that arrive on the local network interfaces that do not match a specific classifier are by default directed to
the default service flow (SF 0). The default service flow is typically a best effort service flow managed with the
DOCSIS request-grant (REQ-GNT) upstream scheduling mechanism. Additional classifiers and service flows may
be present for additional services, such as business services and federated Wi-Fi.
The VoIP (voice over IP) data path from the eMTA (embedded multimedia terminal adapter) is typically mapped to
a constant bit rate service flow (SF 1) that uses the UGS (unsolicited grant service) scheduling service. The VoIP
signaling path is typically sent to a dedicated signaling service flow that uses RTPS (SF 2).
The BWR message contains forward-looking, time-based information that describes to the CMTS upstream scheduler
what the future byte flow is going to be on each BWR flow. The BWR message is typically sent every 1–5 ms
unless there is no traffic on the BWR flows. The service flow that transports the BWR message (SF 3) is typically
configured with UGS or PGS. Alternatively, the BWR could be classified into a common signaling service flow that
uses RTPS (SF 2). Alternatively, the BWR message could be classified into a BWR managed service flow (SF 5),
and the BWR message could be used to schedule its own future bandwidth. That option would allow the entire BWR
data and signaling system to operate independently from the other service flows.
The AE generates its own signaling messages using protocols such as SCTP. These signaling protocols should be
classified to the common signaling service flow (SF 2). Alternatively, the AE could set up a separate BWR flow


32                                                   CableLabs                                               06/23/20
Low Latency Mobile Xhaul over DOCSIS® Technology                                              CM-SP-LLX-I02-200623


(BWR Flow 4) for AE signaling and use the BWR message to create future grants for its own signaling messages.
As another alternative, the CMTS could combine both signaling flows, BWR Flow 4 and BWR Flow 0, into one
service flow (SF 5).
A UE (not shown) collects various layer 3 traffic flows from its applications and classifies them to layer 2 channels, or
LCGs. Typically, LCG 0 is used for signaling, LCG 1 is used for special traffic, LCG 2 is used for higher priority data
traffic, and LCG 3 is used for lower priority data traffic. The LCGs are passed over the air interface from the UE to the
AE. The BWR message describes these LCG flows as they subsequently pass over the network interface to the CM.
The recommended configuration for the DOCSIS CM is to match one CM service flow per LCG. In Figure 17,
LCGs 0 to 3 are described by BWR flows 0 to 3 and are classified to CM service flows 5 to 8. Section 6 describes
how a CM classifier is built in order to get the correct BWR flow to the correct CM service flow. To ensure the
mapping works correctly for the CM classifier, the AE system needs to ensure that a DSCP value cannot be assigned
to more than one LCG. Additionally, the DOCSIS system will discover the source IP address (SA) of the AE (as
described in Section 6). This one-to-one classification allows QoS preferences across LCGs to be preserved in the
DOCSIS system.
It is recommended that all service flows associated with the AE interface (SF 5 through SF 8) be assigned to one
aggregated service flow (ASF) at the CMTS so that the CMTS can set an aggregated maximum sustained rate
(AMSR) that is equal to the AE AMSR. In the event of congestion, the CMTS would use a weighted scheduling
system to ensure that the higher priority queues continue to receive low latency while the lower priority queues deal
with the congestion. The weighting of the queues can be the same as mobile system weighting depending on
configurations determined by the cable operator. Alternatively, the CM could manage the ASF and distribute the
grants across the service flows.
There may be more than one AE behind a CM. In this case, because all traffic is described by its DSCP and IP SA,
the traffic from each of the AEs can be combined onto a common set of service flows in the CM. The DOCSIS
system will need to aggregate the values for each BWR Flow ID in each BWR message from each AE.
If the BWR messages from each AE are members of different 5G mobile slices that each have independent SLAs,
then the CM may have to have an independent set of service flows per 5G slice.

5.4    Cable Modem Grant Sharing
In the backhaul scenario, BWR is intended to accurately predict the amount of data expected to egress on the mobile
AE’s Ethernet interface. But potential error conditions can cause the prediction to be misaligned with the data flows
due to impacts from UE grant sharing, base station packet delay variation (PDV), dropped BWR messages, HARQ
failures, or other phenomena. See Appendix III for detailed discussions of these error conditions. When the CMTS
allocates DOCSIS grants for each DOCSIS SF, the misalignment, when they do occur, can impact DOCSIS grant
utilization or the latency experienced by the misaligned packets. While these conditions are unpredictable, their
impact need to be mitigated.
The discrepancies can be mitigated through the “CM grant sharing” feature, also referred to as “waterfall granting”
in this context. Rather than granting per SF individually and explicitly, the CMTS provides the CM a pool of grants
for the service flows including those related to the BWR protocol. The CM then redistributes the grants to service
high priority flows over low priority flows.
The goal of the CM grant sharing feature is to provide the lowest latency to the highest priority traffic while
ensuring efficient bandwidth utilization, under unpredictable network conditions.
See Appendix IV for a list of use cases intended to be covered by CM grant sharing. To define the feature, the
concept of “enhanced hierarchical QoS” (EHQoS) is introduced in [MULPI]. CM grant sharing feature on the CM
and the CMTS are implemented as the “distributed hierarchical QoS” construct, or DHQoS, within the EHQoS
concept. Detailed requirements are found in [MULPI] Section 7.6, “Hierarchical QoS”. Appendix IV provides an
example of DHQoS applying to BWR flows.

7.5.2    BWR Type 1 Row
Each row is used to request some number of bytes at some future time on a specific BWR flow. Each BWR row
entry includes a BWR Flow ID and the number of bytes requested. The BWR Flow ID is defined via OSS and is
common between the DOCSIS system and the AE. The purpose of BWR Type 1 Row is to convey the Requested

to 0xEF. The value 0x00 indicates there is no differentiation between the flows and that the Flow ID does not need
to be interpreted by the BWR server. The reserved types range from 0xF0 to 0xFD, allowing the option to use 0xFE
to 0xFF to indicate special row extension types.
A BWR Flow corresponds to a flow of packets that can be identified with a set of unique L2 and/or L3 headers that
all traverse the same mobile network interface and share the same QoS.
Typically, there is one BWR Flow ID per flow. In the fronthaul, the flow is designated by the eAxC header in the
eCPRI message. Alternatively, BWR can aggregate multiple flows that are traversing the same AE (backhaul) or RU
(fronthaul) network interface into one BWR Flow ID.
The BWR message can have more than one entry per BWR Flow ID, each entry covering a slot. This allows for a
lower frequency message interval to cover multiple future events within a flow.

The purpose of BWR Type 1 Extension Row is to convey the fronthaul traffic pattern descriptor, encoded as the
BWR Pattern ID, as defined in Table 5. The type 1 extension row can be included after a BWR Type 1 Row. The
AE MUST NOT include more than one BWR Type 1 Extension Row following a BWR Type 1 Row. The AE
MUST NOT include a BWR Type 1 Extension Row without a BWR Type 1 Row.
The Extension Field uses a reserved value of 0xFF to indicate that the current row is an extension row for fronthaul.
The start time of the extension row and the BWR offset of the BWR row together define an interval (e.g., a mobile
slot) whose traffic pattern is described by the BWR Pattern ID field. See Table 5.
The Type 1 Extension Row can also be used to only communicate the start time of the time interval without details
of the traffic pattern, by using BWR Pattern ID value of 0.

7.5.4    BWR Type 2 Row
The purpose of BWR Type 2 Row is to allow the measurement of message latency by the DOCSIS system. The
Type 2 Row can only be included at the end of the BWR report body, with or without the presence of a BWR Type
1 Row. The AE MUST NOT include more than one BWR Type 2 Row per BWR message.
The Extension Field uses a reserved value of 0xFE to indicate that the current row is a special row.
The BWR message generation time (see Table 5) is useful to calculate the transmission time of a BWR message and
the jitter between the BWR messages. The value is the offset from the BWR base nanoseconds value.

7.6     BWR Time Format
The BWR message uses an abbreviated form of the ten-byte PTP timestamp format defined in [IEEE 1588] as a
baseline in its message header. Four bytes are used to count nanoseconds, and four bytes are used to count seconds.

have a minimum resolution of 1.024 μs. With a 16-bit offset field, the maximum resolution is 67.1 ms. The offset
itself is in a power-of-two format that is compatible with the nanosecond counter in the PTP timestamp so that the
two values may be efficiently added together.

7.7    BWR Digital Signature
The BWR signature is an optional feature that prevents a BWR message from a malicious source (which might have
cloned an IP source address) from being accepted. A BWR message is sent in the clear followed by a digital
signature. This permits implementations to use the contents of the BWR message without having to decrypt or
decode each packet. For example, a system could be built in which the BWR message is received in real time, but
the digital signature is sampled on a random basis and not verified in real time.
The full details of the digital signature will be specified in a future version of this specification. The current thought
is to use an AES-128 GCM hash. Certificate authentication may also be included.
It is yet to be decided if the digital signature would also be on the BWR-Beacon-Ack message.

C.3      BWR Discovery and Configuration in Fronthaul
For each BWR Session ID, several associations are required for proper BWR operation:
    •    The CMTS needs to have the association of BWR Session with the corresponding CM interface
    •    The CMTS needs to have the association of BWR Session ID with the corresponding BWR client to which
         it communicates BWR-Beacon-Ack, BWR-Keep-Alive.
    •    The AE needs to have the association of BWR Session ID with the corresponding BWR server to
         communicate BWR reports and keepalives.
Before the RU is installed, it is assumed that the OSS does not know which RU is associated with which transport
system (CM, CMTS). Further, since fronthaul takes a lot of bandwidth on the transmission system, it needs to be a
provisioned service. This section describes at least two ways that the two systems can learn of each other.
In both of these procedures, the transport system comes up and is initialized. This then provides connectivity for the
mobile system to come up and initialize. This implies that there is a least enough bandwidth on the transport system
to provide management plane support between the RU and DU.
The next step is to then associate the mobile system with the transport system. The assumption is that the RU is an
arbitrary unit that gets installed at an RU location.

C.3.1    Manual Discovery




                                        Figure 23 - BWR Manual Discovery
Proposed procedure:
    1.   The installation technician will interface with the OSS, either verbally or through a user interface, and will
         enter identity information for the RU interface and CM interface.
    2.   The DOCSIS OSS knows or is able to determine the association of the CM with the CMTS. The OSS
         system(s) will initialize the DU and the CMTS. The CMTS has the association of the CM interface with the
         RU interface (its BWR Session ID). The DU has the association of RU with CMTS (its BWR server).
    3.   BWR messaging will commence between the DU and the CMTS.
The advantage of this approach is that it is very simple and, depending on how the cross provisioning is performed,
does not necessarily require a coupling between the two OSS systems. The disadvantage is that the system is not
automated.

Appendix II                Comparing LTE and DOCSIS Latency (Informative)
II.1     Introduction
Both LTE and DOCSIS technologies manage a point-to-multipoint network, use a scheduled upstream, and manage
subscriber traffic. The main difference between the two protocols is that the wireless channel and the mobility aspect
of LTE add challenges that are not a concern on the fixed network. However, once connected, scheduling and data
transfers between the UE and the eNB and between the CM and the CMTS follow similar principles, as shown in
Figure 25.




                     Figure 25 - Comparing DOCSIS and LTE Channel Access Mechanisms



II.2     Duplexing
Another difference between LTE and DOCSIS technologies is that LTE supports frequency-division duplex (FDD)
and time-division duplex (TDD). FDD is similar to HFC in that uplink (UL) and downlink (DL) are segregated by
frequency bands. In TDD, however, the same band is used for UL and DL, and the interleaving of the UL and DL
transmissions in the same spectrum adds access delay for UL transmissions. Consequently, the round-trip time
(RTT) for TDD can be much higher than that for FDD.

II.3     DOCSIS Upstream Scheduling
The DOCSIS upstream uses a request-grant approach as shown in Figure 25. A request message is sent from the CM
to the CMTS. The CMTS prepares a DOCSIS MAC management message (MMM), called the MAP (upstream
bandwidth allocation map), and inserts an entry indicating a grant for the CM. The grant entry in the MAP message



54                                                  CableLabs                                              06/23/20
Low Latency Mobile Xhaul over DOCSIS® Technology                                             CM-SP-LLX-I02-200623


contains an upstream service identifier (SID) associated with a service flow assigned to the CM and a transmission
time signaled as a mini-slot number. The CM derives the number of bytes to be transmitted. The CMTS transmits
the MAP to the CM so that the CM can make use of the grant to send its upstream data to the CMTS.
There are many factors that determine the request-grant time in the DOCSIS system; see the DOCSIS specification
[MULPI] for a detailed analysis of the request-grant delay. The minimum delay is basically two MAP intervals plus
some circuit and queuing delay. The DOCSIS 3.0 CMTSs currently use a 2 ms MAP time. One published set of test
results for a Cisco uBR10K CMTS showed a 5 ms minimum request-grant response time [RPHY SCTE].
The CMTS scheduling algorithm can have a significant impact on request-grant delay. For instance, if the CMTS is
using a best effort (BE) scheduling algorithm, then requests needs to be made in contention slots in which the
requests could fail on the first try. If the CMTS is using the RTPS scheduling algorithm, then the request will be
placed in a dedicated slot that ensures the requests are always successful. BE scheduling can perform better than
RTPS when the channel is idle because many request contention slots are available. In that case, BE scheduling
provides lower latency than RTPS. However, in a busy system where there are fewer contention request slots, the
CM will need to re-request often, and RTPS can provide guaranteed latency. Requests can also be sent as a
piggyback message with a data packet; piggybacking is deterministic within a flow and avoids contention.

II.4     LTE Uplink Scheduling
At the heart of the LTE UL scheduler is a similar request-grant mechanism. The request is sent from the UE to an
eNB. The UE is typically a cellular modem such as a cell phone or a dongle, and the eNB is a macrocell or small
cell. When data arrive at the UE, the UE first determines if it already has an LTE UL grant. If it does not, the UE
waits for an opportunity to transmit a scheduling request (SR); typical SR opportunities arise once every 1 to 10 ms,
depending on the configured periodicity of the SR. The purpose of the SR is to keep the upstream signaling
overhead low, which is critical in wireless where spectrum is costly.
Upon receiving the SR, the eNB schedules a UL grant so that the UE can transmit a buffer status report (BSR) to the
eNB. When the eNB receives the BSR from the UE, it becomes aware of the outstanding UL data present in the
UE’s upstream queues. The eNB then schedules UL grants and transmits the grants to the UE through a downlink
control information format 0 (DCI 0) on the physical downlink control channel (PDCCH). LTE operates every
subframe, which is 1 ms. However, because of processing constraints, the eNB’s BSR-grant delay is typically 4 ms
for both the eNB and the UE. In other words, upon receiving the BSR, the eNB performs scheduling and sends a
DCI 0 to arrive at the UE in 4 subframes, and the UE is scheduled to transmit 4 subframes later. Assuming a very
small SR opportunity periodicity (1 ms), the minimum delay before a UE can transmit UL data is 18 ms, which is
slightly longer than the minimum request-grant latency in the DOCSIS system. Detailed request-grant latency
calculations are shown in Table 8.

II.5      Re-transmissions
The LTE transmissions over the air interface often occur in a harsh wireless environment (e.g., cell-edge). Wireless
signals are easily degraded because of path loss and interference, and transmission errors are far more likely in LTE
than in a DOCSIS network. To overcome the transmission errors, LTE has two retransmission mechanisms:
     1.   automatic repeat request (ARQ), which operates at the radio link control (RLC) layer, and
     2.   hybrid automatic repeat request (HARQ), which operates at the MAC and PHY layers.

II.5.1    RLC ARQ
The use of RLC ARQ depends on the type of traffic and the QoS parameters desired (error rate, latency, bit rate,
etc.). For example, for voice over LTE (VoLTE), packet loss is not that critical, but latency is. The human ear can
tolerate small amounts of packet loss, but long delays quickly become annoying during a phone conversation.
Therefore, for voice traffic, ARQ retransmissions are not required, and an LTE EPS bearer for voice can be
configured to use the RLC Unacknowledged Mode (UM) entity, which does not use ARQ. On the other hand, for
TCP traffic, packet loss triggers congestion window reduction, and throughput can suffer. However, longer delays
can be tolerated by TCP traffic so ARQ is used by configuring the LTE EPS bearer to use the RLC Acknowledged
Mode (AM) entity.

II.5.2    HARQ
HARQ is intended to quickly retransmit the LTE transport blocks so that, in conjunction with a good forward error
correcting (FEC) algorithm, LTE can recover from most errors. RLC ARQ, on the other hand, is a higher layer
retransmission mechanism with higher overhead, but it improves the reliability of the link after HARQ.
The purpose of an additional PHY layer retransmission mechanism is to reduce latency. After transmitting a coded
block of bits (a resource block (RB)), the transmitter keeps them in the transmission buffer. The receiver PHY layer
decodes the RB and passes the cyclic redundancy check (CRC) results to the MAC layer. The MAC layer then
issues a HARQ ACK or NACK based on the results. Between the time the RB is received at the receiver and the
time a HARQ feedback is received at the original transmitter of the RB, there is a delay of exactly 4 ms.

II.6      Overall LTE and DOCSIS Latencies
In summary, if we consider only lightly loaded systems and LTE-FDD, then a DOCSIS 3.0 system has a minimum
request-grant delay of about 5 ms, whereas 4G LTE has a minimum request-grant delay of 18 to 24 ms without re-
transmission and 26 to 34 ms with one HARQ retransmission. These latency values will increase under higher loads
or if TDD is used in LTE.

Appendix III               Additional Design Considerations for BWR (Informative)
III.1    BWR Prediction Accuracy on the Backhaul
When the BWR is used, the BWR client tries to describe the data flow across the Ethernet interface by estimating the
data flow across the air interface using the LTE UL scheduler’s grants. Because of potential segmentation and
HARQ failures, the data flow across the air interface may not exactly match the data flow that will occur later across
the Ethernet interface. Other potential errors could occur on the DOCSIS link to contribute to the mismatch. This
section discusses potential error condition. Further details can be found in [BWR SCTE].

III.1.1 Synchronization and Timing
If the LTE system is scheduling in the future and the DOCSIS system is granting in the future, then the timing of
both systems needs to be time synchronized for the grants to line up. One method is to independently synchronize
both the DOCSIS system and the AE to GNSS. An alternate approach is to synchronize the DOCSIS system to
GNSS and use the DOCSIS SYNC framework to allow the CM to generate a 1588 interface that would then be used
to synchronize the AE.

feedback loop to correct any differences between the predicted amount and the amount of data actually arrived.

of granularity. The two framing systems will not be aligned in time, as illustrated in Figure 26. In essence, the eNB

It is often difficult to schedule grants at exact times in a DOCSIS MAP interval because doing so may excessively

III.1.4 HARQ Failures
If the data from the UE is not received correctly at the eNB, the eNB will transmit a negative acknowledgement
(NACK) to the UE, which will trigger data retransmission. Consequently, the grant requested in the BWR message
will not be used for the intended data transfer on the DOCSIS upstream link. The grant can be used by other data, if
other data have been delayed, but the grant might not be used and, therefore, some DOCSIS bandwidth will be
wasted. Refer to [PIMRC] for an upper bound on the unused DOCSIS grant.
Because the LTE scheduler knows when the decoding of a transport block has failed, the LTE BWR client knows
when retransmissions will occur. If the LTE transport block is successfully received when it is retransmitted to the
eNB, some packets may egress from the eNB. The BWR client needs to then account for the delayed and
retransmitted data by adding the byte to a BWR corresponding to the subframe that includes the "re-retransmission."
More details about this operation and its performance can be found in [PIMRC].

III.1.5 RLC Packet Reassembly
The packets that are transmitted from the UE to the eNB are segmented (if required) and placed into LTE transport
blocks. If a packet fits entirely within an LTE transport block, then the UE can transmit it entirely in one subframe.
When the eNB correctly receives a complete packet, the packet can immediately be passed up the eNB’s software
stack and out the eNB’s Ethernet interface.
In the case where not all the segments of a packet have been received, the segments are stored in a buffer at the RLC
layer until all the segments are received. When all the segments are received, the RLC entity reassembles the
segments into a complete packet, and the packet can finally be transmitted out the eNB’s Ethernet interface.




58                                                   CableLabs                                               06/23/20
Low Latency Mobile Xhaul over DOCSIS® Technology                                            CM-SP-LLX-I02-200623


For an example of the effect of segmentation and reassembly, assume that a packet is segmented into two pieces
across two LTE transport blocks and both are received successfully (no decoding failures). The first transport block
is transmitted from the UE to the eNB, but it only contains the first segment of the packet. Because the packet is
incomplete, the RLC entity handling the EPS bearer will store the segment and wait for the next transmission. In the
next subframe 1 ms later, the second transport block is received at the eNB physical layer and, after decoding, the
RLC entity receives the second segment of the packet. The RLC entity reassembles the complete packet, and the
packet is sent out the eNB’s Ethernet interface.
In the example, the packet fragmentation that occurred on the LTE air interface resulted in an additional 1 ms delay
to the time the packet was expected to leave the eNB. Although 1 ms does not seem like much of a delay, consider
that an additional 1 ms delay is added to the packet egress time for each additional segment (e.g., Nfrag − 1 ms), and a
packet that is late even by a small delay may miss a DOCSIS grant, as shown in Figure 27.
Packet fragmentation is one of the causes of mismatches between the bandwidth requested by the BWR message and
the actual data leaving the eNB’s upstream Ethernet interface.




            Figure 27 - Packet Delay Resulting from Packet Segmentation on the LTE Air Interface


Predicting the packet fragmentation and reassembly delay is a difficult problem. The BWR client algorithm is
predicting the future, whereas the reassembly engine works in the present. If the BWR client has packet-level
visibility when scheduling, then it can address this problem. If it does not, then some engineering margin can be
added by the BWR client to the BWR message time index field to capture segmented packets.
It may appear that the solution is for the BWR client to increase the engineering margin in the BWR message.
However, there is a tradeoff between increasing the engineering margin to capture more upstream packets and
upstream latency gain. The engineering margin that maximizes the upstream latency gain of the BWR feature does
not necessarily correspond with the engineering margin that captures 100% of the delayed packets. Careful selection
of the BWR client and engineering margin is needed.

III.1.6 eNB Ethernet Queuing and Transmission Delay
All physical devices have speed and bandwidth limitations and queues to help buffer data when the load on the
interface is high. In a QoS-based system, there will likely be multiple queues serving traffic with different priorities.
The queue depth depends on the traffic, and the time it takes for a packet to arrive at its destination depends on the
amount of time the packet waits in the queue and transmission on the Ethernet interface.
The BWR client needs to allow for queuing and transmission time. If the BWR client knows which QoS level each
packet flow is, it can allocate shorter times for the higher QoS traffic and longer times for lower QoS traffic to
compensate for the time the packet could spend in a queue.
The Ethernet packet transmission time also adds delay, but the transmission delay is small compared to other delays.
For example, a 1,518-byte Ethernet packet on a gigabit interface only takes about 12 μs to transmit.

III.1.7 Signaling Traffic

III.1.7.1            Real-Time Signaling Traffic
LTE signaling can be categorized into three general categories, listed below. This generalization applies to all
signaling packets that leave the Ethernet interface of the eNB, whether they are intended for another eNB or for the
evolved packet core (EPC).
     1.     Fully Scheduled Signaling: UE signaling traffic transmitted to the network and fully accounted for by the
            eNB scheduler.
     2.     Partly Scheduled Signaling: eNB signaling transactions that have one or more transactions to the UE
            followed by one or more transactions to the Ethernet interface. This signaling is only partially accounted
            for by the eNB scheduler.
     3.     Unscheduled Signaling: Signaling traffic originating at the eNB and not accounted for by the eNB scheduler.
The BWR client’s prediction algorithm will have to account for each of these three cases. The eNB may know about
the upcoming non-UE-originated signaling. The BWR client can then account for the additional bytes in the BWR
message.

III.1.7.2            Periodic Signaling Traffic
Many signaling messages are time critical and needs to be transmitted immediately. Some signaling messages may
be periodic and quite predictable.
Periodic signaling messages are interesting because the CMTS can issue grants in a similar way as the eNB issues
semi-persistent scheduling (SPS) grants. For example, instead of repeatedly requesting 100 60-byte grants for voice
packets arriving in the UE’s transmit queue every 20 ms, LTE issues a single SPS grant to the UE for 100 60-byte
grants spaced out by 20 ms. As long as the signaling traffic is predictable and timely, an SPS version of the BWR
could be used by the eNB to ask the CMTS for periodic grants of a given size. The benefit of the SPS-BWR would
be a reduction in BWR messaging overhead.

III.1.8 BWR Prediction Error
The BWR client’s prediction algorithm that creates the BWR message at the eNB is constantly trying to predict the
upstream traffic that will egress from the eNB several subframes in the future. Despite the BWR client’s best efforts,
as explained in Appendix III.1.4 and Appendix III.1.5, HARQ failures and packet segmentation can cause
differences between the amount of expected data and the actual amount of data that leaves the eNB. In addition,
timing errors such as poor synchronization, DOCSIS grant jitter, and packet egress jitter can cause the packets to
miss the grants. The accuracy of the BWR client algorithm and error correction algorithm could be differentiating
aspects for eNB and CMTS vendors.
For instance, on the eNB side, an in-depth understanding of the eNB’s own software processes is a good start to
developing a good BWR client algorithm that creates accurate BWRs. For example, if hard, real-time code cannot
be written to ensure that packets egress at an exact time, knowing the timing statistics for the LTE UL stack and the

Low Latency Mobile Xhaul over DOCSIS® Technology                                         CM-SP-LLX-I02-200623


Ethernet device can be helpful in predicting when packets will egress the eNB. Calibrating the eNB UL stack is
possible and has been done in a proof of concept.
For the DOCSIS system, the CMTS can observe the predicted behavior and actual DOCSIS grant usage. The CMTS
can then help to manage the error by either increasing the buffer delay in the CM or by actively managing the buffer
on the CM and sending extra grants when the buffer increases in size.
When errors do occur, the BWR itself can be used to address inaccuracies. For instance, when decoding of the
transport blocks fails, the eNB re-requests enough bytes for the future retransmission.
The BWR client could be even more proactive and monitor the amount of data leaving the Ethernet device. When the
actual upstream data differ from the predicted upstream data, the eNB could communicate this difference to the
CMTS by subtracting the bytes from the BWR offset it originally requested and adding the bytes to a new offset.
The BWR client should adjust its requested byte count based on any additional encapsulation bytes that have
occurred between the mobile air interface and the Ethernet interface. Figure 28 illustrates an example of where

the BWR client does not have visibility at the packet boundary level at the UE, it cannot accurately subtract the
various headers that the UE included in the BSR (PDCP, RLC, MAC), which can result in the eNB over-predicting

III.1.9 BWR Message Flow
The BWR is a signaling message that is sent to the CM via the Ethernet interface. Depending on the CM queuing
configuration, the BWR message may need to be assigned a separate QoS level from the data. If the BWR message
is classified to a separate queue on the CM with an RTPS or unsolicited grant service (UGS) flow, then the byte
count of the BWR message is not accounted for in the service flows that carry the EPS bearers. If the BWR is given
high priority and placed on a common service flow, then it has to be accounted for.
The LTE-DOCSIS system may have to account for the network bandwidth used by the BWR message, depending
on the CM queuing configuration.

III.1.10 BWR Message Delay
If the BWR message is predicting 8 ms in the future, then in theory, it has 8 ms to complete its journey from the
eNB to the CMTS and for the CMTS to issue a grant. In practice, the CMTS will need the BWR message before it
runs its upstream scheduler and MAP builder routine. That MAP routine is run early depending on the MAP
advance time that the CM requires. If the MAP interval is 2 ms and the MAP advance time is 2 ms, and if some
margin is added, then the BWR message might need to arrive 5 ms early. If the eNB takes 1 ms to generate and
transmit the BWR, then only 2 ms of margin is available.
For this reason, it is important that when the BWR message arrives at the CM, it is transmitted to the CMTS as
quickly as possible. Additional latencies, such as contention in a request slot, will cause the BWR to arrive at the
CMTS too late. To avoid contention delays, DOCSIS scheduling algorithms such as RTPS or UGS can be used.
Because the BWR message can vary in length, UGS may not be the best choice; the size of the BWR would have to
be fixed or the UGS grant made larger than or equal to the length of the longest BWR message. Additionally, more
advanced scheduling mechanisms might be needed at the CMTS.

III.1.11 BWR Message Loss
One option for sending BWR messages over Ethernet and the DOCSIS link is UDP encapsulation. Although UDP
adds little overhead compared to TCP, UDP should not be considered reliable.

III.1.11.1        Improving BWR Reliability
To make the LTE-DOCSIS system more robust, the BWR includes a sequence number to help the CMTS detect a
dropped BWR message. Note that if the eNB has no data to send, it does not send a BWR message to indicate
processing time, indicated as BWR offset, and expected egress bytes. Therefore, the absence of the BWR offset and
its associated requested bytes alone could not be used to detect dropped BWR messages.
Many communications systems use retransmissions to ensure data are received at the receiver. In the case of a
dropped BWR message, retransmission is not a viable solution. Because the system has a margin of only 2 ms (see
Appendix III.1.10), there is not enough time to detect BWR message loss and also retransmit the original message.
A better solution may be to duplicate the requested bytes per BWR offset and BWR flow within a BWR message
over "N" BWR messages. The number of duplicate messages "N" can easily be configured at the eNB, but the
proposed value for N is three. Duplicating the requested bytes over three messages keeps the total bandwidth
requirements low while helping to increase the reliability of the LTE-DOCSIS system. In the current version of
BWR messages, duplication is not required.

III.1.12 DOCSIS Grant Duplication Due to CM Requests
With the introduction of BWR, there are now two request mechanisms—the external Layer 3 BWR request
mechanism and the DOCSIS native Layer 2 CM request mechanism.
When data packets from the eNB arrive at the CM, the CM generates a DOCSIS request message (REQ) if the grant
from the BWR is not immediately available at the CM. If the CM BE flow requests its own grant, then two grants
could be generated for the same data, one from the REQ and one from the BWR. This outcome could lead to wasted
bandwidth if the duplicate DOCSIS grants are not used.
The CM BE flow request behavior is dependent on the CM hardware implementations and can be hard to predict. As
an example, during Cisco/CableLabs R&D efforts with a specific CM in the testbed [BWR SCTE], that CM was
observed to not issue a duplicate request when the BWR was scheduled and received by the CM within 1–2 ms of
upstream data arriving at the CM.
In theory, because the BWR message contains all the request information needed for the data path, the additional
CM BE flow requests could be disabled or ignored. In practice, mismatches between the BWR’s predicted
bandwidth request and the actual upstream data from the eNB can occur, and the CM BE flow requests can be useful
in managing buffer build-up.
The solution is to keep the CM BE flow requests enabled and ensure that the BWR is scheduled in a timely manner
to prevent the CM from making duplicate requests. This practice ensures good synchronization and accurate



62                                                   CableLabs                                               06/23/20
Low Latency Mobile Xhaul over DOCSIS® Technology                                             CM-SP-LLX-I02-200623


prediction of when the data will leave the eNB and arrive at the CM. In addition, an intelligent CMTS algorithm can
be developed for this purpose to detect and ignore duplicate CM BE flow requests.

III.1.13 Rogue AE
In order for the BWR concept to work well, there needs to be a certain level of trust between the eNB and the
CMTS. For instance, the CMTS does not know how much data the eNB truly needs to allocate the UE(s), so it needs
to trust the eNB—to an extent. For example, a rogue eNB that wants to reduce its latency as much as possible could
intentionally request more bandwidth than it needs, at the expense of wasted DOCSIS grants and, potentially, the
QoS experienced by other customers. A poorly designed BWR prediction algorithm could also put an increased
burden on the DOCSIS upstream. Whether the BWR inaccuracy is intentional or unintentional, a smart CMTS BWR
processing implementation will have checks to ensure the eNB is not abusing the BWR.
A simple method to ensure that the BWR is not abused is to check if the upstream data flow matches the grants
requested. If a CMTS measures the eNB’s grant utilization and finds that it is using all the grants it asks for, then the
BWR is not being abused. On the other hand, if an eNB is requesting significantly more bandwidth than it needs,
then the CMTS would find that it is using only a fraction of the grants it asks for. When poor utilization or other bad
behavior is observed, the CMTS could flag the eNB and enforce restrictions, such as by limiting the maximum
traffic or dynamically scaling back the size of the grants based on the grant utilization of the eNB in question. An
operator or a CMTS implementation could define how much utilization constitutes bad behavior.
This approach puts some responsibility on the eNB’s BWR prediction algorithm because it needs to predict the
upstream egress data flow with reasonably good accuracy or else its bandwidth will be limited by the CMTS.

III.2    BWR Prediction Accuracy on the Fronthaul
Appendix III.1.8 identifies potential factors that can contribute to the differences between the amount of data
requested and the amount of data actually received by the CM for the backhaul case. Some of those factors do not
apply for the fronthaul case.

III.2.1 HARQ Failures
Because the HARQ process terminates at the O-DU, all IQ data need to be carried through the DOCSIS fronthaul link
whether the HARQ process is a success or a failure and whether or not a retransmission is needed. As such, there will
be no mismatch between the requested bytes from the BWR message and the actual data that egress the O-RU.

III.2.2 RLC Packet Reassembly
Because the fronthaul link carries the IQ data rather than the complete IP packet, there will be no mismatch between
the requested bytes from the BWR and the actual data that egress the O-RU.

III.2.3 Real-Time Signaling Traffic
Because the higher layer LTE stack is located in the O-DU, northbound of the CMTS, the O-DU does not need to
include partially scheduled and unscheduled signaling in the BWR.

III.2.4 BWR Message Loss
For fronthaul, BWR messages are sent from the DU directly to the CMTS on the Ethernet network without going
through DOCSIS network. Message loss is therefore less likely to occur in fronthaul than in backhaul. Duplicate
messages might not be needed.

III.2.5 DOCSIS Grant Duplication Due to CM Requests
This topic is for further study.

III.2.6 Rogue AE
This topic is for further study. A rogue AE may still occur in fronthaul, although the likelihood is lower than in
backhaul. The DU usually resides at the network edge in a secure place.

III.3    Impacts of BWR Implementation
If the BWR is generated every millisecond, then the AE has to generate 1,000 messages a second that it did not have
to generate before. This process may require additional CPU resources.
To compare actual data versus predicted data, the AE would have to monitor its own Ethernet output and timestamp
packets in real time. It then could compare offline the time alignment of the actual packet to the predicted values.
Adding these functions may have some hardware impact.
The CMTS has to create a routed path for the BWR from an external entity to the scheduler instance.
The CMTS scheduler process will have to receive 1,000 BWR messages a second per connected AE. If there are two
AEs per DOCSIS service group (SG) and 32 SGs per line card, then the CMTS would receive 64,000 more
messages per second than it receives now. Disabling the CM request flow for the queues under use can help reduce
the number of messages that the CMTS needs to receive and process.
The throughput for transporting the BWR messages from an AE could be on the order of 1.25 Mbps of traffic. If
there are many AEs in a CMTS domain, then this bandwidth usage could be noticeable. Additional traffic could also
impact the control plane in the CMTS.
The CMTS may want to check the percentage of unused grants for a AE. Unused grants could be a result of
improper prediction or over-requesting. Results of a grant audit can be combined with the error vector from the AE
to generate statistics.
Generating BWR messages at the optimal 1 ms interval places additional load on the LTE eNB scheduler. There are
two ways to alleviate the computation requirement on the scheduler.
     •   Increase the interval that the BWR messages are sent. However, the maximum latency gain might not be
         achieved if the BWR message interval is too large.
     •   Decouple the BWR information generation from the BWR message generation; the two processes can be
         asynchronous. Doing so would allow the LTE scheduler to report the expected egress bytes at the optimal
         interval (i.e., 1 ms) without overloading the eNB with the message generation process. The BWR message
         format (see Section 7.1) is designed to indicate multiple egress times.

Appendix IV                CM Grant Sharing Use Cases (Informative)
As seen in Appendix III, for the backhaul deployment scenario, potential error conditions can cause the BWR
prediction to be misaligned with the data flows, thereby impacting the DOCSIS latency and grant utilization. The
CM grant sharing feature can be implemented to mitigate the impact on the DOCSIS link. Under CM grant sharing,
rather than granting per SF individually and explicitly, the CMTS grants the aggregate service flow (ASF) which
includes the service flows related to the BWR protocol. Once the CM receives the grants for the ASF, it redistributes
the DOCSIS grants to service high priority flows over low priority flows.
The goal of the CM grant sharing feature is to provide the lowest latency to the highest priority traffic while
reducing the impact on grant utilization under unpredictable circumstances.
This section discusses a list of use cases intended to be covered by CM grant sharing.

IV.1     Common QoS Framework for Mobile and DOCSIS
This use case, as shown Figure 29, illustrates the common QoS framework between the mobile and the DOCSIS
systems, and provides the basis for CM grant sharing.




                                       Figure 29 - Common QoS Framework


The DOCSIS link is subject to congestion. An eNB backhauled on the DOCSIS network cannot be given its own
dedicated bandwidth. It needs to share bandwidth with other users during the times of congestion.
During congestion, the DOCSIS system grants high priority queues first, while allowing the lower priority queue to
build-up and potentially packet drops. To do so, the DOCSIS system needs to differentiate the traffic with different
priorities.
Mapping each LCG to a separate DOCSIS SF allows the DOCSIS system to match the QoS expectations of the
mobile air interface. Separate SFs also allow the CMTS to fairly allocate bandwidth of same priorities across
multiple CMs.

IV.2     Mobile UE Grant Sharing
The UE requests per LCG and receives a block of grants which it then allocates across LCGs. It is possible that
between the request and grant, additional traffic can arrive at the UE. UE grant shares by reallocating the grants

IV.5     Mobile HARQ Bandwidth Reuse

IV.7     PGS Grant Reuse
Instead of serving the eNB signaling queue with RTPS, PGS scheduling service can be used to further reduce the
latency. PGS comes at a high cost of bandwidth efficiency. To provision for the PGS service, assumptions need to
be made about the signaling traffic pattern:
    •    Assume one 100-byte packet arrive at 1ms, the PGS flow takes up 0.8 Mbps of bandwidth
    •    Assume two 1500-byte packet arrive at 0.5 ms, the PGS flow takes up 50 Mbps of bandwidth
The actual signaling traffic pattern is difficult to predict. Therefore, the cost of PGS is likely somewhere between the
two data points above. However, it is important to note that, if the queue is empty, then all of this bandwidth will be
wasted without an alternate plan.
CM grant sharing allows PGS grants that are otherwise wasted to be utilized by sharing with all other queues.
In the example shown in Figure 35, PGS grants allocated for SF 4 are shared with SF 0 to SF 3. When grants are
delivered for the other queues, they can return a grant back to SF 4. CM grant sharing results in less grants wasted
and low latency for all the flows.
Conceptually, CM grant sharing benefits not only mobile xhaul but also residential broadband services.

Appendix VI                  LTE Overview (Informative)
LTE (long-term evolution) is a standard for high-speed wireless data communication for mobile devices. Because LTE-
based 4th generation (4G) networks provide data at speeds almost 10 times faster than before, 4G has revolutionized
the wireless communication sector. LTE technology was developed to be a global standard by 3GPP that would
replace 3G connectivity.
The 4G LTE connectivity can be deployed in two modes: LTE frequency-division duplex (LTE-FDD) and LTE
time-division duplex (LTE-TDD). The use of either duplex mode is driven by the amount and type of spectrum band
that is available for deployment.
The two key differences between LTE-FDD and LTE-TDD are how data is uploaded and downloaded and the
frequency spectrum used to deploy the networks. LTE-FDD uses paired frequencies for two-way data communications:
one frequency channel is used for data upload (uplink) and a separate one is used for data download (downlink). LTE-
TDD uses a single frequency, alternating between uploading and downloading data. The ratio between uploads and
downloads on an LTE-TDD network can be configured based on the deployment use case, i.e., on whether more data
needs to be sent or received. LTE-FDD and LTE-TDD also operate on different frequency bands. 3GPP has defined
the operating bands for LTE modes in the specification [TS 36.101].

VI.2     LTE QoS Overview
This section provides an overview of the end-to-end QoS framework in the LTE evolved packet system (EPS). The
EPS consists of the LTE air interface and the LTE EPC. The QoS framework spans the entire EPS.
The LTE EPS incorporates a flow-aware QoS mechanism consisting of two interconnected levels. At the service
level, a service data flow (SDF) consists of a group of IP flows associated with a service, and at the bearer level, an
EPS bearer is used to transport SDFs with the same QoS class.
There are two types of EPS bearers: default and dedicated. A default bearer is established between the UE and the
PGW when an UE initially attaches to the network. This is similar to the DOCSIS concept of primary service flow
established during CM registration. When additional services with different QoS are initiated, a dedicated bearer is
established for each additional QoS class.
A QoS class is defined by two parameters: QoS class indicator (QCI) and allocation and retention priority (ARP).
They are applied to all SDFs and EPS bearers. The QCI defines QoS characteristics including resource type,
priority, packet delay budget, and packet error loss rate. It controls packet forwarding treatment (e.g., scheduling
weights, admission thresholds, queue management thresholds, link layer protocol configuration) at network nodes.
The ARP controls call admission attributes consisting of priority level, pre-emption capability, and pre-emption
vulnerability. ARP is not the focus of this specification. The two resource types defined by the QCI value are GBR
(guaranteed bit rate) and non-GBR. Every EPS bearer is assigned a QCI value, which is classified as GBR or non-
GBR based on the QCI.

VI.2.1 EPS Session and Bearers




                                               Figure 42 - LTE Bearers


An IP connection between a UE and a packet data network (PDN) is referred to as a PDN connection or EPS session.
An EPS bearer is a logical connection between a UE and a PGW through which IP packets are delivered over the LTE
network. A UE can support up to 11 EPS bearers simultaneously. EPS bearers are identified by their EPS bearer ID.
An EPS bearer is concatenated with three bearers, each connecting two adjacent nodes in the LTE EPS: a data radio
bearer (DRB), an S1 bearer, and an S5/S8 bearer.
The DRB, identified by a DRB ID, is used to carry user data traffic over the radio link between a UE and an eNB.
The DRB ID is assigned by the eNB and is mapped with EPS bearer IDs on a 1:1 basis.
An S1 bearer is established between an eNB and an SGW and is carried through a GTP tunnel. (GTP is the GPRS
Tunneling Protocol; GPRS stands for General Packet Radio Service.) S1 bearers are identified by the GTP tunnel
endpoint identifier (TEID). Similarly, an S5/S8 bearer is established between an SGW and a PGW through a GTP
tunnel and is identified by the same TEID.

VI.2.2 The Radio Bearer
On the air interface, a DRB is carried in a logical channel (LC) using a 1:1 mapping. On the UE, one or more LCs of
similar QoS profiles are mapped into a logical channel group (LCG). Rather than requesting bandwidth for each LC,
the UE requests bandwidth for each LCG, helping reduce the request-grant overhead on the air interface, which is
resource-limited. It is important to note that whereas other concepts such as the LC that extend beyond the eNB have
relevance in backhaul transport (e.g., the LC and DRB have a 1:1 mapping and are part of an EPS bearer), the LCG
exists on the air interface only, to facilitate a more efficient request process.
The eNB sends a single grant, to be shared among the LCGs reported by the UE. In theory, the UE needs to fill the
grant for the LCGs with descending priority. However, it is possible that a packet belonging to a higher priority LC
arrived between the request and the grant. In this case, the UE will prioritize sending the higher priority packet even
though it did not request bandwidth for it. This function can result in some lower priority packets not being sent
even though the UE had requested bandwidth for it.
LTE handles QoS differently compared to the DOCSIS network.
     •   Responsibility for the QoS on the air interface is shared between the UE and the eNB. The eNB handles the
         QoS at the LCG level, using information that is at least 4 ms old. The UE makes real-time scheduling
         decisions by allowing a newly arrived, higher priority DRB packet to go first.
     •   The eNB does not have knowledge of how many bytes belong to which LC. Its knowledge is coarse
         compared to the precise knowledge held by the CMTS.
When the eNB receives a packet on an LC, it forwards the packet on its Ethernet interface encapsulated in the
corresponding GTP tunnel.

In the uplink direction, IP flows from user applications are filtered in the UE using UL TFTs and mapped to a

Appendix VII               5G Overview (Informative)
Previous generations of mobile technology were focused on connecting people through smartphones. With increasing
emphasis on IoT and autonomous and low-latency systems, 5G focuses on connecting everything. To meet this vision,
developers and operators have set ambitious targets—data rates up to 20 Gbps and capacity increased 1,000 fold. The
new technologies needed to achieve these targets include new spectrum, new antenna technologies (known as massive
multiple-input multiple-output (MIMO)), network slicing, and new network architecture.
5G has built-in support for cloud implementation with open RAN and edge computing. Low latency requires
bringing the content close to the radio, which leads to local break out and multi-access edge computing (MEC), and
the need for scalability requires bringing the benefits of the cloud to the radio networks. 5G radio and core is also
designed for native cloud implementation, including new interfaces inside the radio network.

VII.1 5G Use Cases
One of the main design goals for 5G has been to introduce flexibility in mobile systems to support use cases with
diverse sets of requirements ranging from enhanced mobile broadband (eMBB) to massive machine-type
communication (mMTC) and even ultra-reliable low-latency communication (URLLC). This flexibility creates
possibilities for new kinds of use cases for mobile communication, and many new use cases are already emerging
for 5G that are driven by different verticals.
High-reliability, low-latency mobile communication has in particular raised strong interest in the industrial domain,
e.g., in the context of IoT, where the aim is to connect and integrate industrial control systems with enterprise
systems, business processes, and analytics. To achieve this integration, powerful and pervasive connectivity between
machines, people, and objects is necessary. 5G is capable of meeting this need, and it makes 5G an important
enabler for many IoT promises, such as including flexibility of production, new advanced mobile applications for
workers, and collaboration between mobile robots and autonomous vehicles on the shop floor, among others.

VII.2 5G QoS
The 5G system provides a flow-based QoS framework to enable per-packet QoS treatment for DL and UL traffic
across mobile and transport networks. User traffic is carried in QoS flows that are dynamically established to meet
the specific QoS needs of mobile services. 5G Core and UE functions enforce the applicable QoS rules on individual
QoS flows and at aggregate levels to ensure the 5G system provides the expected QoS behavior.
The 5G QoS framework incorporates many aspects of the LTE QoS framework, with the following key differences.
    •   Each packet of a QoS flow is marked with a QoS flow ID (QFI).
    •   A 5G QoS Profile defines the set of QoS characteristics to be applied to a QoS flow.
    •   Non-standardized QoS characteristics can be dynamically signaled at session establishment.
    •   Reflective QoS enables a UE to derive the QoS characteristics of a UL QoS flow from the DL QoS flow.
    •   A notification control mechanism is introduced to alert the 5G system when the QoS parameters for a
        guaranteed bitrate (GBR) flow cannot be met.
The operation of 5G QoS for UL traffic is illustrated in Figure 45. The User Plane Function (UPF) of the 5G Core
establishes one or more PDU sessions with each UE. A single PDU session contains one or more QoS flows of a
Service Data Flow (a collection of IP flows associated with a service).




                                            Figure 45 - 5G Uplink QoS


The gNB establishes at least one DRB with the UE for each PDU session. For PDU sessions with multiple QoS
flows, the gNB may setup multiple DRBs and map each QoS flow into the appropriate DRB according to the QoS
characteristics. QoS flows with similar QoS characteristics may be mapped into a single DRB. The gNB also maps
the QoS flows to the GTP tunnel established for the UE. The UPF provides the IP flow to QoS flow classification
rules to the UE. For non-GBR flows with reflective QoS enabled, the UE derives the QoS rules from the associated
DL QoS flow. Multiple IP flows with similar QoS characteristics may be mapped into a single QoS flow. Packet
filters in the UPF associate the QoS flows with a service data flow (SDF). The UPF enforces the Session-AMBR for
non-GBR QoS flows within each PDU session and the UE-AMBR rate for all non-GBR QoS flows in all PDU
sessions of a UE.
The 5G QoS Profile is a set of QoS characteristics to be applied to a QoS flow. A QoS Profile typically includes
Resource Type, Priority Level, Packet Delay Budget, Packet Error Rate, Maximum Data Burst Volume, and Averaging
Window. For GBR flows, the QoS Profile may also contain the Delay Critical parameter. The QoS Profile also
provides the guidelines for setting gNB-specific parameters (e.g., RLC, MAC, HARQ) on a per QoS flow basis.
The QoS Profile contains a descriptor indicating whether the QoS characteristics are dynamic or non-dynamic. In the
dynamic case, the QoS characteristics are signaled by the UPF to the gNB during PDU session setup or during QoS
flow setup/modification. In the non-dynamic case, the QoS characteristics may be inferred from the standardized QoS


06/23/20                                            CableLabs                                                     81
CM-SP-LLX-I02-200623                                              Data Over Cable Service Interface Specification


classes defined for LTE (and identified with a QoS Class Indicator). However, some of these QoS characteristics can
optionally still be signaled for dynamic "adjustment." Each QoS Profile is identified with a unique 5G QoS Indicator
(5QI). In the non-dynamic (standardized) case, the 5QI matches the corresponding QCI value.
For each QoS flow, the UPF assigns a unique QFI and the selected QoS Profile to be applied to the QoS flow. Each
packet of a QoS flow is marked with the QFI so that the appropriate QoS treatment can be applied. A GTP extension
header called the UL/DL PDU Session Information frame carries the QFI of the QoS flow to which the packet
belongs.
The optional notification control mechanism can be enabled for GBR flows. If the "notification requested" flag is set
in the QoS Profile, then the gNB notifies the UPF if the GFBR (Guaranteed Flow Bit Rate) cannot be fulfilled for
the QoS flow. Upon receiving the notification, the UPF notifies the application, which may decide to take corrective
action such as reducing the required QoS level. Because the gNB remains in control of the DRB resources, it could
choose to release the resources if there is no reply from the UPF.

VII.3 Performance Requirements
The first use case of 5G is enhanced data rates and boosted capacity for mobile broadband applications. 5G enables
very high data rates by using wideband carrier and multiantenna transmissions. The typical maximum bandwidth
below 1 GHz is 20 MHz FDD, which enables 200 Mbps peak rate with 2×2 MIMO. The mid-band deployment with
100 MHz TDD and 4×4 MIMO allows peak rates up to 2 Gbps and millimeter waves at 24–28 GHz up to 10–20 Gbps
by using 1 GHz of bandwidth and 2×2 and 4×4 MIMO.
When used on top of existing LTE networks, 5G brings substantially more capacity with wideband carrier and
massive MIMO. An LTE cell with 20 MHz bandwidth shows 40 Mbps average busy-hour throughput in live
networks. A 5G cell with 100 MHz bandwidth enables 20 times more capacity by using 5 times more spectrum and
achieving at least 4 times more efficiency with massive MIMO. 5G at mid-bands is the biggest capacity boost on
macro cells to date.
Among 5G’s characteristics is that it can provide both low latency and high reliability. The target latency for 5G
ranges from 10 ms for most applications to 1 ms for the most demanding. Achieving such low latency requires new
radio frame design, new device and base station hardware, and, in practice, local content or break out with MEC and
local cloud. Low latency is typically combined with ultra-high reliability for new use cases such as robot control.
A 5G network is designed to support diverse and extreme requirements for latency, throughput, capacity, and
availability. Network slicing is one solution to meeting the requirements of all use cases in a common network
infrastructure. The same network infrastructure can support, for example, smartphones, tablets, virtual reality
connections, personal health devices, critical remote control, or automotive connectivity. 5G network slicing can be
used to ensure that end-to-end performance meets customer expectations, as well as service and application
requirements. To make use of network slicing properly, the individual segments including radio, transport, metro,
core, edge cloud, and central cloud, which were formerly treated separately, need to be examined as a whole.
Performance optimization also needs to be adapted and coordinated across the entire network.
A 5G network requires tools for network slicing. LTE supports quality of service (QoS) differentiation, but more is
needed in 5G, namely dynamic, application-based quality of experience (QoE). A solution based on an LTE bearer
is fine for operator-provided services in which the packet filters are easy to define and application sessions are long
lived. 5G QoE architecture, however, needs to detect and differentiate short-lived subservice flows. The control
plane signaling of packet filter attributes and related policies is not necessary when both the radio and the core are
application aware and can make rapid decisions on actions to achieve the QoE targets.

Appendix VIII              Virtualized RAN (vRAN) Overview (Informative)
A virtualized radio access network (vRAN) enables operators to improve the scalability and performance of their
wireless networks and provides opportunities to optimize the network architecture to meet the increasingly challenging
service requirements. For example, delay-critical functionalities can be localized closer to the users, and less delay
critical functionalities can be located in the edge cloud. Such a solution can minimize transport requirements and
lower costs.
The disaggregation of RAN functions and the specification of interconnection interfaces enable virtualization of the
RAN. 3GPP and other groups have defined an approach for separating the protocol functions of the NodeB (Figure 46)
into high and low subfunctions; they have identified several options for splitting these functions into separate vRAN
entities.




                                 Figure 46 - NodeB Control Plane Protocol Stack




                                    Figure 47 - RAN Functional Split Options
                                  (Modified from 3GPP [TR 38.801], figure 11.1.1-1)


The choice of how to split NR functions in the architecture depends on some factors related to radio network
deployment scenarios, constraints, and intended supported services. Examples of such factors are listed below:
    •    need to support specific QoS settings per offered services (e.g., low latency, high throughput),
    •    need to support specific user density and load demand per given geographical area (which may influence
         the level of RAN coordination), and
    •    need to function with transport networks that have different performance levels, from ideal to non-ideal.
The following vRAN functional entities have been defined by 3GPP based on Option 2 in Figure 47.
    •    RU: Remote unit; contains all lower PHY functions.
    •    DU: Distributed unit; contains all higher PHY functions, as well as MAC and RLC.
    •    CU: Central unit; contains all RAN functions above RLC (PDCP and SDAP). The CU also terminates
         inter-RAN interfaces (X2, Xn). For 5G NR, the CU is subdivided into user plane (CU-UP) and control
         plane (CU-CP) functions.

IX.1     Fronthaul over DOCSIS Transport
For fronthaul, a transport system interconnects the DUs with their RUs. Many different topologies can be encountered
(one or more DUs connecting to one or more RUs with one or more interfaces on each RU) and needs to be supported.
The different interconnection options between DUs and RUs are shown in Figure 48, assuming the simple case of
point-to-point links:
     •   A deployment consists of one or more RU nodes and one or more DU nodes.
     •   Each DU node has one or more schedulers (dark blue dots in the figures in this appendix).
     •   Each RU node has one or more radio "cells" (carriers or sectors) (light blue dots in the figures in this
         appendix).
     •   Each radio cell in an RU node is managed by one DU scheduler in a 1-to-1 fashion.
     •   Each RU node can have one or more network interfaces.
     •   Each RU node can generate one or more fronthaul flows. A fronthaul flow is the set of fronthaul packets
         pertaining to a radio cell on a RU node on a network interface and a unique set of QoS or class of service
         (CoS) requirements. The QoS/CoS requirements can be used to differentiate user plane flows from control
         plane flows or to differentiate within the user plane traffic.




                                      Figure 48 - RU-DU Fronthaul Topologies


Deploying a DOCSIS system between the DUs and RUs means that each RU network interface is connected to a
CM interface. This leads to even more interconnection options, depending on which RUs are connected to which
CMs, and the CMs themselves pertaining to which CMTSs.
The concept of BWR is that there is cooperation between the DU scheduler and the CMTS scheduler for allocation
of upstream transport network bandwidth to the fronthaul flows from the RUs. A unique identifier is needed in the
BWR interaction for each specific fronthaul flow in order to be able to differentiate between the flows. As described
in Section 7.2, the full identifier is composed of the BWR Session ID plus the BWR flow ID.

Appendix X                  802.11ax/ay Overview (Informative)
Unlike previous generations of 3GPP standards, the 5G network is designed to operate with different wireless
protocols. Specifically, it is possible for a Wi-Fi access point to provide 5G services to clients with some limitations. It
is expected that the client devices will be a part of the 5G network and receive 5G services such as secure admission,
roaming, operator network access, security, and privacy. Other medium-specific features may be limited for a Wi-Fi
client; in particular, reliability and QoS-related features can be provided in a limited manner. [IEEE 802.11ax] and its
corresponding high-frequency companion, [IEEE 802.11ay], provide improved air-time management and significantly
improve the channel QoS aspects. 802.11ax is the next generation of 802.11ac, operating in frequencies up to 6 GHz
and providing high throughputs and relatively good coverage. Similarly, 802.11ay is the next generation of 802.11ad,
operating at much higher frequency bands around 60 GHz and providing ultra-high throughputs with coverage limited
to line-of-sight and shorter ranges. 802.11ax is the version of the IEEE standard that corresponds to Wi-Fi 6. A Wi-Fi 6
certified device supports 802.11ax.

X.1      802.11ax/ay Major Improvements
802.11ax/ay focuses on improving the network efficiency in dense, multi-client environments. In such environments, it
offers improvements in network efficiency 4 to 6 times that of legacy standards with mechanisms described below. The
peak throughput for 802.11ax is increased by 40% compared to 802.11ac.
At the physical layer, the new standards use OFDMA and multi-user MIMO for both uplink and downlink. Longer
symbols and higher modulations are also introduced in 802.11ax/ay. At the MAC layer, a completely new channel
access scheme is introduced, with centralized control of the AP.
Because 802.11ay shares the same MAC features of 802.11ax, and because the 802.11ax standard is much more
advanced, the sections below refer mostly to 802.11ax.

X.2      Legacy Channel Access
Unlike licensed spectrum protocols, Wi-Fi assumes that a user will have neighbors and that they may operate with
any generation of Wi-Fi on overlapping frequency bands. In previous generations of Wi-Fi, the air medium is not
centrally managed and channel access is performed by a simple listen-before-talk protocol with random backoff in
case of collision detection. The protocol applies equally to clients and access points. Some prioritization of traffic is
made possible through the Wi-Fi Multimedia (WMM) protocol. WMM introduces several priority levels known as
access categories. The higher the priority level, the more aggressive the transmitter with its channel access
parameters, e.g., its random backoff range. Channel access with WMM is a simple mechanism that has operated
well for many years because it is not only simple but also interoperates well with neighboring networks. As Wi-Fi
networks became denser and more use cases emerged, a simple listen-before-talk protocol was no longer sufficient,
and there was a need to revise Wi-Fi channel access.

X.3      802.11ax/ay Channel Access Overview
802.11ax/ay is the first generation of Wi-Fi to introduce scheduling as a key feature of channel access. The 802.11
AP owns periods of air time in which it schedules upstream transmissions for its clients and also transmits data to its
clients. WMM is updated to operate under the concept of a scheduled channel. 802.11ax/ay clients may report to the
AP on their buffers or indicate on load, and the AP is expected to grant them accordingly. The unlicensed air
medium is still a chaotic medium where neighboring networks continue to operate on overlapping frequency bands,
with some of them using legacy versions of Wi-Fi. 802.11ax/ay scheduling, therefore, operates only when it has won
the competition for air time.
802.11ax introduces multi-user MIMO for both downstream and upstream. It also introduces OFDMA for both
upstream and downstream. With these two techniques, the AP can communicate with multiple clients
simultaneously on both directions, so scheduling becomes a two-dimensional task.

X.4.2    Client Buffer Status Reporting
The AP and the clients communicate in order to transmit and receive data. This section discusses primarily the
upstream communication protocol. Each packet is classified at the client to a traffic identifier (TID). Each TID holds
a specific QoS context and parameters. The client queues packets for transmission based on their TIDs; logically,
there is one queue per TID. The clients report on their buffer size per TID to the AP through several techniques. On
every transmission, a client may include a buffer status report (BSR) element reporting on the depth of the buffers
for each of its TIDs. This BSR may come in a transmission of data MPDUs (the equivalent of a DOCSIS piggyback
request) or in a null payload packet (the equivalent of a DOCSIS REQ).
A more efficient way for the AP to poll for its clients’ buffer status is through a mechanism called the NDP
Feedback Report Poll (NFRP). The NFRP trigger frame is a short frame sent by the AP asking clients to report on
their status. The answer is a very short feedback that may include only a single bit field reporting on the buffer
status. If the field is missing, the client’s corresponding buffer is empty. If the field is set, the buffer is non-empty
but below a configured threshold. If the bit is set, the buffer is above a configured threshold. An AP may poll
"silent" clients from time to time to efficiently sense their buffer status.

X.4.3    Triggering
The upstream time allocation is called triggering. A trigger frame is sent by the AP to instruct the clients on when to
transmit, which subcarriers to use, and how long to transmit. The trigger frame can therefore be seen as the 802.11ax
equivalent to the DOCSIS MAP. The addressed clients respond to the trigger frame almost immediately. Failure to
respond will leave the air unused and will allow other devices to take control of it. With OFDMA, multiple clients
can transmit on the same channel simultaneously with different subcarriers assigned to each client. Each
transmission is responded to immediately with a block ACK by the receiver.
Figure 54 shows an example of a triggered transmission (preambles are not shown). The AP sends a trigger frame
that is received by all clients that are currently awake (see Appendix X.4.4 for power save overview). The trigger
contains transmit allocations for a list of clients. Immediately after receiving the trigger frame, the clients transmit
data in parallel over allocated groups of subcarriers. The AP acknowledges the clients in parallel by using an
OFDMA block ACK.

X.4.4    Target Wakeup Time
802.11ax introduces powerful power saving through a mechanism called Target Wake Time (TWT). Each client
wishing to use this feature negotiates its TWT parameters with the AP. Parameters include wake-up time, minimal
wake-up duration, and wake interval. While not awake, the client may shut down its receiver. When wake-up time
arrives, the client is expected to wake up and be ready to receive data and trigger frames from the AP. The AP
adheres to the TWT parameters and cannot transmit or trigger the client for transmission outside of the wake
interval. TWT constrains the AP scheduler in order to coordinate transmission time to a client’s wake-up periods.
